## 1. Principles

1. **Know whether you are default alive before anything else.** The first diagnostic for any company older than 8-9 months is whether current spending and current revenue growth reach profitability on the cash left. If yes, talk ambition; if no, talk rescue. Start asking early, because asking too early costs little and asking too late is fatal. Founders mostly do not know their own answer. (aord, 2015) Rests on P13, P9.

2. **Separate facts from hopes, and write down plan B.** "We are default dead but counting on investors" should set off alarms. Investor interest is fickle even with great growth, so fundraising is only ever plan A. Write down what you do if the money fails and the date you switch. (aord, 2015; startuplessons, 2006; really, 2009) Rests on P9.

3. **The fatal pinch is a trap with a time limit.** Months of cash, heavy burn and weak growth, combined with an assumption that more money will arrive. It is self-reinforcing: slack about profitability lowers your odds of raising. Second-round raising is harder than the first because you spend more, are held to higher standards, and now read as a failure by default. (pinch, 2014) Rests on P9, P10.

4. **Treat each round as your last.** The less you need further investment, the easier it is to get. Act as if the money you just raised is all you will ever get. (pinch, 2014; really, 2009: "assume you won't get money, and if someone offers any, assume you'll never get more"; but if offered money, take it) Rests on P9, P8.

5. **Hiring too fast is the main killer of funded startups.** Founders think headcount produces growth; he thinks big staffs are mostly the effect of growth. Overhiring also makes the product harder to change, and early on the product needs to evolve rather than be built out. Investors may push you to overhire because their incentives (portfolio, kill-or-cure) differ from yours (survival). (aord, 2015; startupmistakes #12, 2006; pinch, 2014) Rests on P3, P10.

6. **Cheap beats everything while you search.** Most startups fail by running out of money before making something people want, so being cheap is nearly the same as iterating fast. Low cost also makes you recession-proof ("cockroach"). (13sentences #8, 2009; badeconomy, 2008; startupmistakes, 2006) Rests on P1.

7. **Ramen profitability changes your position.** Covering founders' living costs means you no longer face raise-or-die. It improves terms, makes you more attractive to investors, lifts morale, and removes the fundraising distraction. It is a way not to die en route, not the destination. (ramenprofitable, 2009; really, 2009) Rests on P1, P8.

8. **Growth rate is the single number and the compass.** A startup is a company designed to grow fast; the one number every founder should know is the rate (a ratio, not a count of new customers). Pick a weekly rate you think you can hit, hit it, and judge every decision by it. Growth pressure can even find the idea. (growth, 2012) Rests on P13.

9. **Small differences in growth rate compound into different worlds.** Exponentials defeat intuition; at startup rates a modest-looking weekly number becomes huge in a few years, and slow growers tend not to reach big size. This is why he asks for growth first and why a few-million company at 93% per month is about nine and a half months from a billion. (growth, 2012; earn, 2026) Rests on P13.

10. **Optimize growth, not extraction.** Being soft on pricing only shifts the curve by a constant factor (a fixed number of weeks behind), while a small gain in growth rate overtakes it. Founders can safely be nice because making great things compounds and rapacity does not. Exception: when growth itself depends on extraction, or when survival to profitability depends on per-customer revenue. (safe, 2015) Rests on P13, P6.

11. **Kick-start growth by doing things that don't scale.** Recruit early users by hand, delight them extravagantly, start in a narrow market, even be the software manually. Big launches and partnerships usually fail as growth starters; the effort has to be extraordinary, not the event. Every startup is fragile early; the danger is dismissing your own startup. (ds, 2013) Rests on P6, P1.

12. **Survival is mostly about not quitting, and the cause of death is usually morale.** The official causes (cash, a founder leaving) usually sit on top of demoralization. A handful of users who love you gives you something to expand and keeps you going. Distraction ("we'll keep working on the startup, but...") is a signal of giving up. (die, 2007; 13sentences #11-12, 2009; startuplessons #5, 2006) Rests on P12.

13. **Persist on the goal, flex on the path.** Determination is the top founder quality, but stubbornness about specific ideas is lethal. Attach to the goal high in the decision tree, give way on details low down when expected value drops. The test: do you get most engaged when someone disagrees? (persistence, 2024; relres, 2009; startupmistakes #5, 2006; startuplessons #5, 2006) Rests on P12.

14. **Make a few users love you before many are lukewarm.** Satisfy all needs of a subset rather than some needs of all. It is easier to expand in users than in satisfaction, and user count is harder to lie to yourself about than "85% done." (13sentences #5, 2009; die, 2007) Rests on P22.

15. **Attention is a survival resource; keep money and disputes from becoming the top idea in your head.** Fundraising and acquirer talks stall work because they dominate ambient thinking. Avoid distractions that pay: day jobs, consulting, side projects. (top, 2010; 13sentences #10, 2009; ramenprofitable, 2009) Rests on P12.

## 2. Concrete tests and numbers

|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | **Default-alive test.** Assume expenses stay constant and revenue growth stays at the rate of the last several months. Do you reach profitability on the money left? Ask once the company is past roughly 8-9 months. Trevor Blackwell's calculator (growth.tlb.org) computes it. Half the founders PG talks to do not know their answer. | aord, 2015 | P13 |
| T2 | **Fatal pinch signature.** Significant cash but heavy monthly loss, with growth zero or mediocre; example runway given as 6 months. Default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix. | pinch, 2014 | P13 |
| T3 | **Probability of raising when already in the pinch: "zero"** (his deliberately stark estimate, a rhetorical device). If an existing investor seems to promise more and runway is 8 months or less, try to get the money now so you learn the truth. | pinch, 2014 (note 2) | P9 |

| T5 | **Revenue levers when pinched.** Put everyone on selling; change the question from "do you want our product?" to "what would you pay a lot for?"; sell a precisely defined derivative of your product, keep the IP, no hourly billing; be ruthlessly mercenary, make customers pay a lot, quickly. | pinch, 2014 | P6 |

Usage growth needs a higher rate to impress than revenue growth, because investors discount predicted revenue. | aord, 2015 (+ note 1) | P9, P10 |
| T7 | **Runway for a second raise / plan B date.** Write down precisely what you do if you can't raise and exactly when you switch to plan B. | aord, 2015 | P9 |

| T9 | **Weekly-to-yearly table:** 1% = 1.7x, 2% = 2.8x, 5% = 12.6x, 7% = 33.7x, 10% = 142x per year. | growth, 2012 | P13 |
| T10 | **Four-year illustration:** $1,000/month at 1% weekly becomes about $7,900/month after 4 years; at 5% weekly about $25 million/month. A company at 1.7x a year would need roughly 19 years to match 4 years at 5% weekly. | growth, 2012 (+ note 10) | P13 |
| T11 | **Growth is a rate, not a count:** "100 new customers a month" is not a rate; constant absolute additions mean falling growth rate. Revenue is the best thing to measure; active users are next best if you aren't charging. Facebook/Twitter-style products need a higher user growth rate since users are a proxy. Watch for high churn that gives good net growth until you run out of potential users. | growth, 2012 (+ notes 7) | P13 |
| T12 | **Set a target rate you think you can hit, and hit it every week.** Miss it and you should be correspondingly alarmed. Everything that gets the rate is "ipso facto right" (excluding trickery: buying users above lifetime value, counting inactive users as active, rationing invites to fake a curve). | growth, 2012 (+ note 8) | P13 |
| T13 | **Compounding from ds:** at 10% weekly, 100 users becomes about 14,000 after a year and about 2 million after two. To grow 10%/week at 100 users, add 10 next week. | ds, 2013 | P13 |
| T14 | **Nice-founder arithmetic:** $1,000/month growing 5% weekly is about $160k/month after two years. Extracting only half the value gives $80k, and you are always 15 weeks behind (since 2 is about 1.05^15). Half the extraction but 6% weekly growth gives about $214k/month vs $160k after two years, and about $4.4M vs $2M/month a year later. | safe, 2015 | P13 |
| T15 | **Billion-dollar arithmetic:** 93% monthly growth needs log base 1.93 of 500, about 9.45 months, to grow 500x (from an assumed $2M to $1B). At 15% monthly for 5 years: 1.15^60 is about 4,384x; $10k/month becomes about $44M/month (about $526M/year). Only two inputs matter: growth rate and how long it lasts. Real rates slow, but the outcome is similar. | earn, 2026 | P13 |
| T16 | **Expected value of a founder's bet:** a $100M outcome at 1% chance is $1M expected value; probability for exceptional founders may be 20-50%. Judge startups by the mean outcome, not the median. | growth, 2012 | P13 |
| T17 | **YC survival base rates (2007 talk):** expected about a third of funded companies to succeed, hoping for half. About 10 of the first batches' startups had died. A startup going silent for a couple of months, then not replying to email, was a "100% accurate" predictor of death in that sample. The odds of getting from launch to liquidity without some disaster: "one in a thousand." | die, 2007 | P12 |
| T18 | **Fundraising size and speed rules:** take 50-100% more than you think you need (software and deals take longer); raise enough to reach the next visible step (prototype, launch, significant growth); if a reputable firm offers a reasonable valuation with no onerous terms, take the first reasonable deal. Fundraising time grows with amount. If you are raising, productivity may fall to about a third of normal and the process can take months. | startupmistakes #11, #13 and notes 5, 2006; ramenprofitable, 2009 | P10 |
| T19 | **Hiring rules (three):** don't hire if you can avoid it; pay with equity rather than salary partly to attract committed people; hire only people who will write code or get users. | startupmistakes #12, 2006 | P3 |
| T20 | **Ramen-profitable definition and example:** revenue just covers founders' living expenses; example of $3,000/month after 2 months with two 25-year-old founders. A ramen-profitable startup "may be more likely to succeed than not." | ramenprofitable, 2009 | P1, P8 |
| T21 | **Time horizon:** successful startups usually take at least 3 years and probably 5+; deals and development take 2-3x longer than founders expect. Only about 1 in 100 successful startups has an instant-success trajectory. | really, 2009 | P12 |
| T22 | **Deals heuristic:** about 20 deals fell through at Viaweb; after the first ~10 they treated deals as background processes. "Don't get your hopes up" whenever someone says "we want to invest in you" or "we want to acquire you." Keep looking for other investors after a verbal interest. | 13sentences #13, 2009; startuplessons #7, 2006 | P9 |
| T23 | **Co-founder attrition stat:** about 20% of YC-funded startups (as of 2006) had a founder leave; hence he advises vesting. | startupmistakes #17, 2006 | P12 |
| T24 | **Luck model:** outcome is the product of skill, determination and luck, so a zero on any factor gives zero. | really #16, 2009 | P12 |
| T25 | **Top-idea test:** take a shower and notice what your thoughts return to. If it is money or a dispute rather than the product, change your situation. | top, 2010 | P12 |
| T26 | **Persistence test:** do you get more engaged when someone points out a flaw? Are you attached to the goal or to your first ideas on how to reach it? | persistence, 2024 | P12 |
| T27 | **Convergence test for pivots:** if each new idea reuses most of what you built for the previous ones, you are probably converging; if you restart from scratch each time, that is a bad sign. Users excited about the new direction is a good sign. | startupmistakes #5, 2006 | P22 |
| T28 | **"Does anybody love it" test:** at least some users must be ecstatic about it. If zero, tweak and try again; if a small group, grow that core. Blogger and Delicious took years from such a core. | die, 2007 | P22 |
| T29 | **One-or-two-sentence test:** you should be able to say what you do in one or two sentences, to everyone. | startuplessons #3, 2006 | P22 |

## 3. Questions he would ask the founder

1. If your expenses stay flat and revenue keeps growing at its recent rate, do you reach profitability before the cash runs out? Show me the calculation. (aord, 2015)
2. What is your weekly growth rate, and is that ratio or an absolute number? What rate are you targeting and did you hit it last week? (growth, 2012; startuplessons, 2006)
3. If investors say no, what exactly do you do, and on what date do you switch to that plan? Is it written down? (aord, 2015)
4. Are you counting on a round that has not closed? Which of your current beliefs are facts and which are hopes? (aord, 2015; startuplessons #7, 2006)
5. Why are you hiring? Would growth be faster with a hire, or are you avoiding the thought that the product is not appealing enough? (aord, 2015; startupmistakes, 2006)
6. Is there anyone who really loves what you've built? What do they love, and where could you find more people like them? (die, 2007; 13sentences, 2009)
7. What are you doing manually, by hand, to get users and make them happy? Who did you personally recruit this week? (ds, 2013)
8. What are you doing in the company that isn't the startup (day job, consulting, grad school, side projects)? Which of them is paying you enough to interrupt your work? (die, 2007; 13sentences #10, 2009)
9. What did you change about your idea in the last few months, and did it reuse what you'd already built? (startupmistakes #5, 2006; really #10, 2009)
10. What does your thinking drift to in the shower? (top, 2010)
11. Do you and your cofounder have a relationship that can survive being "married" to a failing company? Did you suppress any misgivings when you chose them? (startupmistakes #17, 2006; really #1, 2009)
12. When someone points out a hole in your plan, do you lean in or glaze over? (persistence, 2024)
13. What is the market size you could grow into if the growth rate held, and what is your real churn? (earn, 2026; growth, 2012 note 7)

## 4. Failure patterns he warns about

1. **The overhire death spiral.** Moderately appealing product, decent early growth, easy first round, a hiring spree to "boost growth," growth doesn't come, costs are high, investors lose interest, company dies. (aord, 2015)
2. **The fatal pinch.** Months of runway, big burn, mediocre growth, plan to raise again; slack about profitability reduces the odds of raising. (pinch, 2014)
3. **Hope as plan.** Counting on investors to save a default-dead company; leaning the company on a deal that will probably fall over; reading a casual investor remark as a commitment. (aord, 2015; pinch note 2, 2014; startuplessons #7, 2006)
4. **Premature scaling.** Scaling a small business with bad unit economics to get impressive numbers; makes the business harder to fix and burns cash fast. (aord note 4, quote from Paul Buchheit, 2015)
5. **"But we're going to keep working on the startup."** Grad school, consulting, a move, side projects: euphemisms for giving up, with a face-saving alibi. (die, 2007; 13sentences #10, 2009; startupmistakes #18, 2006)
6. **Drifting into consulting.** Ramen-profitable via contract work feels like progress but you are not a product company; customers' demand for custom work always pulls you down the slope. (ramenprofitable, 2009; pinch, 2014: acceptable as a rescue if mercenary and bounded)
7. **Demoralization and silence.** Going quiet is the visible sign; the underlying cause is exhausted morale, not literally unpaid bills. (die, 2007; really #3, 2009)
8. **Obstinacy.** Reflexive refusal to change ideas, attached to first ideas rather than the goal; includes the version where a team keeps building what "everyone will love" regardless. (persistence, 2024; startupmistakes #5, startuplessons #4, 2006)
9. **Ignoring users.** The worst of three self-inflicted killers (internal disputes, inertia, ignoring users) and a bigger threat than competitors. (startuplessons #4, 2006)
10. **The big launch and the partnership.** Embargoed multi-outlet launches and big-company deals as growth strategy; both take more effort than expected and return little. (ds, 2013)
11. **Raising too much or too little.** Too little cannot reach the next visible step; too much sets a clock, brings hiring pressure and hardens direction, and the raise itself eats months. (startupmistakes #11-13, 2006)
12. **Fearing the wrong things.** Worrying about Google rather than unknown startups; treating visible disasters as fatal. (startuplessons #4, 2006; really #11, 2009)
13. **Vanity growth.** Buying users above lifetime value, inflating active-user counts, rationing invites to fake a smooth curve; throws off your own compass. (growth note 8, 2012)
14. **Fundraising as the top idea.** Once it takes over the shower thoughts, product work stalls. (top, 2010; ramenprofitable, 2009)

## 5. How his view changed over time

- **2006 (startupmistakes, startuplessons):** Spending too much "is not as common as it used to be" and few funded startups overspend. Main worry is raising too little; recommends taking the first reasonable VC deal and raising 50-100% more than expected. Fear of competition is mostly misplaced. Founder quality (determination) is the key variable.
- **2007 (die):** Survival is framed around morale and not giving up. Little about cash arithmetic; the idea is mainly "keep hearing from you, keep typing."
- **2008 (badeconomy):** Economy is "rounding error" compared to founders; act now; make the startup cheap (the "cockroach" idea). Notes investors become more reluctant in recessions.
- **2009 (ramenprofitable, 13sentences, really, relres):** Introduces a concrete financial target (ramen profitability) and explains why it hacks the investment process. Cheapness is now paired with profitability as a position rather than just thrift. He explicitly rejects "bootstrapping" as a rule: few startups succeed without taking investment, so ramen profitability does not mean never raising. In 13sentences, profit-paying side work counts as a worse distraction than other types. In really he quotes a founder regretting not assuming no further money would come, and repeats his advice to assume none will.
- **2010 (top):** Adds the mechanism for why fundraising and disputes hurt: they take over ambient thinking. This explains earlier observations (die, ramenprofitable) rather than changing advice.
- **2012 (growth):** Shift from survival emphasis to growth as the organizing metric. Gives numeric benchmarks (5-7% / 10% weekly). Earlier essays said "launch and listen to users"; now "pick a growth rate and hit it weekly." He calls this turning a startup into an optimization problem.
- **2013 (ds):** Qualifies the "build it and they will come" hope: founders must manually recruit. Consulting-style effort is endorsed here only while you are not paid by the hour (compare ramenprofitable 2009 and pinch 2014).
- **2014-2015 (pinch, aord):** The balance shifts. In 2006 spending too much was rare; by 2014-2015 overhiring is "by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money." The new concept is a diagnostic (default alive/dead) and explicit advice on firing and consultingish revenue. Reason he gives: the pattern is now common enough to name, and funded founders are being encouraged to overhire. He also now says the investor-interest threshold is growth above about 5x a year, but never to be relied on.
- **2015 (safe):** Reframes pricing: optimize growth rate, not extraction. Notes the counter-case (surviving to profitability) but says the underlying cause of death is usually slow growth or excessive spending, not under-monetization.
- **2024 (persistence):** Refines "determination" (2006) into five parts (energy, imagination, resilience, judgement, goal focus) and separates it sharply from obstinacy. Same underlying claim, more detailed.
- **2024 (foundermode):** New in the scaling phase. Standard advice to hire good people and let them run things is wrong for founders; he is explicit that founder mode is not yet understood and warns it will be misused. Relevant to survival only for the later "scaling" stage.
- **2026 (earn):** Restates the growth mathematics in billion-dollar terms. Notes 15% per month is "not rare at all"; compare with the 2012 weekly benchmarks (5-7% per week is about 23-33% per month).

## 6. Where his advice is time-bound

Ledger status given for each item. "PG text" marks things he says; "Inference" marks my reasoning.

**Cheapness, ramen profitability, "spend little" (P1 amplified; P3 amplified)**
- PG text: ramen profitability only "recently became feasible" because software is cheap; not feasible for biotech-type startups (ramenprofitable, 2009). Startups "keep getting cheaper" (startupmistakes, 2006).
- Inference: with AI coding tools, the argument gets stronger for application companies: fewer people are needed to reach the first revenue and founders can reach ramen profitable earlier. The risk is that building is no longer evidence of commitment, so ramen profitability matters more as proof that anyone will pay.

**"Money can't make software get built faster" and the cost of raising (P2 reversed for some)**
- PG text: his advice to raise little and spend little assumes extra money mostly buys headcount that slows you (startupmistakes #12; aord). He also notes exceptions where "progress" substitutes for revenue (pinch note 1).
- Inference: for companies training models or buying heavy compute, spending is not a pure drag. Default-alive math still applies, but "spend little" needs to be split into people cost and compute cost. For application companies on top of models, the old premise mostly holds.

**Cost per user (P5 weakened)**
- PG text: default-alive arithmetic is about expenses held constant (aord); ramen profitability assumes founders' living costs are the main cost (ramenprofitable).
- Inference: with AI inference costs scaling with usage, "expenses remain constant" is false when growth itself raises cost. The default-alive test needs cost per active user in it; heavy users can reduce margins and the "growth is free money" intuition fails. His safe (2015) argument that under-extracting costs only a constant-weeks delay assumes costs don't rise with usage; if each user costs real money, being "nice" or under-pricing can fail to catch up.

**Distribution and growth benchmarks (P6 weakened)**
- PG text: word of mouth from a great product spreads (safe, earn, startuplessons); but he also says founders must recruit users manually (ds) and notes one founder's criticism that YC underplays customer acquisition cost (really #12).
- Inference: when building is cheap and channels flood, "make something great and growth follows" is less reliable, and the weekly benchmarks (5-7%, 10%) probably measure the wrong population (they came from YC batches in the early 2010s). Expect more products with spiky growth that churns, which makes his own warning in growth note 7 (churn) more important. The do-things-that-don't-scale advice gets stronger.

**"Spending is largely unrelated to growth" and the product edge (P7 weakened, PG has said so)**
- PG text (via ledger): in 2026 he moves toward "sources of power" rather than a better product. In aord (2015) he says fast growth usually comes from hitting a nerve rather than money spent.
- Inference: if a copy appears fast, high early growth can be temporary. Treat growth as evidence of a need but not of durable position; ask what keeps the growth once clones arrive.

**Investor behaviour (P9 holds; P10 holds)**
- PG text and ledger: the investor-fickleness, never-count-on-a-deal and parallel-raise advice stays good.
- Inference: the numbers (5x per year threshold, 50-100% more than you need, first-reasonable-deal) were set in a different market (see P11) and may be low for current AI-company rounds. Keep the reasoning, refresh the inputs.

**Who has fundraising leverage (P8 split)**
- Inference: "ramen profitable means you are no longer at the mercy of investors" matters most for lean application companies, which now have more leverage; capital-heavy model companies depend on a few very large investors and may not be able to become ramen profitable at all. Before using his fundraising rules, check which kind of company the founder runs.

**Big companies as competitors (P15 split)**
- PG text: big companies are not the ones to fear, even Google (startuplessons #4, 2006).
- Inference: for products that depend on compute, data or distribution, incumbents are now a more serious threat than he claimed; the "fear other startups" rule is weaker there.

**Cheaper failure, more attempts (P20 amplified)**
- PG text: he already notes more people starting startups makes competitors invisible (startuplessons #4).
- Inference: more simultaneous copies increases the chance that growth is contested, so speed to growth and power matter more than idea priority. "Don't worry about competitors" (really #11) needs a caveat: competitors are likelier and appear faster, though the "they usually have a different goal" point still holds in many cases.

**Period specifics (P11 dated)**
- Specific details tied to 2006-2015: YC-batch benchmarks, "$3,000 a month" ramen, Windows/Java platform mistakes, "a third to a half of YC startups succeed," the 2008 recession analysis. Use as examples of reasoning only.

**Parts that hold (P12, P13, P22):** the compounding arithmetic, the logic of default alive, morale, persistence vs obstinacy, the cost of distraction, and the preference for a few loving users hold; only the input numbers change.

## 7. Limits and tensions

1. **Survive vs. grow.** Advice to be cheap, get ramen profitable and avoid hiring pulls one way; "growth is the compass" (growth, 2012) and the benchmarks pull another. He tries to reconcile it by saying spending and growth are barely connected (aord), which is asserted, not demonstrated. Also, ramen profitability's "main significance is buying time" (2009) sits awkwardly with the claim in growth that growing slower is dangerous because competitors will grow.
2. **Take the first reasonable deal vs. keep looking.** startupmistakes #13 (2006) says take the first reasonable VC offer and don't bargain-hunt; startuplessons #7 (2006) says keep looking after an investor shows interest, because you'll get better terms. The two are reconcilable (look until you hold a real offer) but he doesn't say so.
3. **Raise more vs. act as if it's the last money.** In 2006 he warns about raising too little and says take 50-100% extra; in 2014-2015 he warns raised money brings overhiring pressure. The reconciliation is to raise generously but spend as though you had none; this is implied, not stated outright.
4. **Consulting.** Ramenprofitable warns against drifting into consulting; pinch recommends consultingish work as a rescue; ds says consulting-like effort is fine only if you aren't paid by the hour. The rule that holds across the three is "keep a single product, bound the custom work," but the lines are judgement calls.
5. **Distractions that pay.** 13sentences calls profitable side income the worst distraction, yet ramenprofitable praises reaching revenue early. The difference is whether the income is from the product itself.
6. **Evidence base.** Most numbers come from YC companies and his own experience (about 70 startups per 6 months in really, 2009). Several claims are explicitly guesses: "the probability is zero" (pinch), "100% accurate predictor" of death from a small early sample (die), "nine times out of ten sitting around strategizing is procrastination" (growth), and the guess about the band of people who would have succeeded if they had quit their jobs (startupmistakes #18, where he says "I'm guessing here"). He admits VCs may be wrong that overhiring is in their interest (aord note 3, "my guess").
7. **Survivorship in "don't give up."** He says that if you keep trying you will find a variation that works (die), and that hard work is usually enough provided you keep changing the idea (13sentences). This ignores that persistence can lead to the long slog of a bad business; the counter-weight is the pinch advice to shut down if certain of failure and the explicit "luck is a big factor" (really #16). The persistence essay (2024) adds a way to tell the two apart but doesn't give a stopping rule.
8. **Growth benchmarks without a time frame.** 5-7% per week for a short YC window is not the same as sustained rates; earn (2026) uses 15% per month as "not rare." He says rates slow, but gives no rule for when slowing signals trouble.
9. **Flags of uncertainty he makes himself.** Founder mode: "we still don't know" what it consists of and predicts misuse (foundermode, 2024). Growth: "startup is a pole, not a threshold." Hardware, biotech and "progress" companies are exceptions to ramen profitability and the revenue-growth test (pinch note 1; ramenprofitable).
10. **Silence on what to do with moderate-growth, profitable businesses.** Everything assumes the goal is a fast-growing startup (growth, 2012). A default-alive company growing at 1-2% a week is by his own definition not on the startup path, but he does not say whether it should be kept going; ramenprofitable says that's not the destination, and growth note 10 says slow growers rarely get big in technology.
