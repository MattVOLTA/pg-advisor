## 1. Principles

**1. Start from a problem you actually have, not from a startup idea.**
- Essays: startupideas (2012), organic (2010), bronze (2005), startupmistakes #4 (2006), earn (2026), google (2024).
- His reasoning: trying to think up startup ideas produces ideas that sound plausible but have no real demand ("made-up" or "sitcom" ideas). Working on a problem you have guarantees the problem exists, and the most common startup mistake is solving problems nobody has. The verb he wants is "notice", not "think up". Wozniak wanted a computer; Houston forgot his USB stick.
- Rests on: P12, P22.

**2. Live in the future, then build what is missing.**
- Essays: startupideas (2012), before (2014), organic (2010), ideas (2005), earn (2026).
- His reasoning: people at the leading edge of a fast-changing field (even as users, not only builders) see gaps others cannot yet see, so their needs predict future demand. Good ideas come from an external stimulus hitting a prepared mind. The practical recipe is to become the sort of person who has such ideas, which in the worst case takes a year.
- Rests on: P12, P21 (taste/hunches), weakly P14.

**3. Demand shaped like a well: a few people who want it a lot beats many who want it a little.**
- Essay: startupideas (2012).
- His reasoning: nothing large that many urgently need can be built at version-one effort without already existing, so you must compromise. Choose narrow and deep. Nearly all good ideas have this shape, though not all well-shaped ideas are good, because you also need a path out to adjacent markets (Harvard to all colleges; Altair Basic to operating systems).
- Rests on: P22, P13.

**4. The best ideas look like bad ideas, and most ideas that look bad are bad.**
- Essays: swan (2012), altair (2015), newthings (2008), startupideas (2012), earn (2026).
- His reasoning: if a good idea were obviously good, someone would already have done it. Apple, Microsoft, Facebook, Airbnb and Google all looked lame or wrong at launch. He quotes Thiel's Venn diagram: the sweet spot is the intersection of "seems bad" and "is good", but the overlap is small. His test for a lame-sounding idea is to ask "what Microsoft is this the Altair Basic of?" and treat it as a puzzle, not as a joke.
- Rests on: P12, P9 (investor herding), P22.

**4a. Corollary on how to apply principle 4 without fooling yourself.**
- Essay: startupideas (2012, fn 2), swan (2012), ambitious (2012).
- His reasoning: the set of plausible-sounding ideas is far bigger than the set of good ones, so "sounds plausible" is zero evidence. Being dismissed as a toy is positive evidence only when real users love the thing now. He does not claim that sounding bad makes an idea good.
- Rests on: P12.

**5. Turn off the schlep filter and the unsexy filter.**
- Essays: schlep (2012), startupideas (2012), ambitious (2012), startupmistakes #3 and #16 (2006), bronze ("muck", 2005).
- His reasoning: your unconscious hides ideas that involve painful or distasteful work, and almost every programmer has the same blindness, so the convenient ideas have been picked clean. Stripe is the example (payments: banks, fraud, regulation). A scary idea has less competition, like an undervalued stock. A good idea will have as many schleps as the one you avoided anyway. Antidote: ignorance, or ask "what do I wish someone else would solve for me?"
- Rests on: P12, partly P15 (incumbents too slow or timid). Note: schlep-filter logic is also about P1 (see section 6).

**6. Where there is muck there is brass: unpleasant work pays, and the aim must be to make money, not to be cool.**
- Essays: bronze (2005), startupideas (2012).
- His reasoning: work people like pays badly because of supply. If you are doing something cool, the aim should be to make money and maybe be cool, not the reverse; it is too hard to make money by accident. Hackers can learn to ask what customers want and it is easier than the problems they are used to solving.
- Rests on: P12.

**7. A crowded market is a good sign if you have a thesis about what everyone else overlooks; do not fear competitors.**
- Essays: startupideas (2012), startupmistakes #3 (2006), bronze ("hyenas", 2005), ideas (2005).
- His reasoning: competitors rarely kill startups (so rare you can almost discount it). Inexperienced founders over-credit them. Hiding in a marginal niche to avoid competition just avoids good ideas. Exception he names: a competitor with lock-in that stops users from choosing you. Best thesis: incumbents lacked the courage of their convictions (Google, whose better results made users leave faster).
- Rests on: P15 (partial), P12.

**8. Treat the first idea as a question, not a blueprint, and discount for sunk time.**
- Essays: ideas (2005), bronze ("still life effect", 2005), startupmistakes #5 (2006), swan (2012, fn on ideas), ambitious (2012).
- His reasoning: startup ideas are not worth anything on their own (there is no market for them). Most startups end up unlike the initial idea; its main value is that discovering it is broken leads to the real one. Hours invested do not make an idea good. Plunge in, but revisit it "in the harsh light of morning". Do not hold on like an Olympian (obstinacy), and do not flip every week either.
- Rests on: P12, P20.

**9. Start deceptively small; expand "westward" like Columbus rather than building to a blueprint.**
- Essays: ambitious (2012), startupideas (2012), newthings (2008), ideas (2005), altair (2015), earn (2026).
- His reasoning: big ambitions take longer and the further you project, the more likely you are wrong. Start with something that works for a small, intense group (Basic for the Altair; Harvard), find the path out later. He recommends not announcing the grand goal ("just say you are building todo-list software") so you do not raise expectations or attract haters. Fast route out requires a beachhead that is similar to the larger market.
- Rests on: P22, P13.

**10. Make something people want is the only real failure axis; everything else is secondary.**
- Essays: startupmistakes (2006 intro, #15), before (2014), bronze (2005), earn (2026).
- His reasoning: nearly every cause of failure funnels into not making something users want. Tricks (fundraising, growth hacks) are an order of magnitude less important. Making something people want is much harder than monetising it, so leave the business model for later but never ignore it. Growth rate is the first question he asks because it shows whether you built the right thing.
- Rests on: P22, P16.

**11. Ask what would make the company more powerful, not just richer.**
- Essay: powerful (Sep 2026).
- His reasoning: asking how to make more money yields increments; asking what makes the company more powerful can make it orders of magnitude more valuable. Power routes he lists: own the customer relationship; make money flow through you; create an app-store or marketplace so others build on you; induce network effects (even by letting users opt in to share data or train the model); go full stack; eat through the customer by doing all their hard work; watch for a peripheral "tail that wags the dog" (PayPal); help users make money; play the long game; be generous; offer APIs (agents are replacing human users); sell to early-stage customers who decide fast and then grow with you; get the data early (Rippling); go "upstream".
- Constraint he stresses: every power move must make things better for the customer, or there is no uptake.
- Rests on: P7 (he explicitly shifts from "better" to "powerful"), P22, P6, P15.

**12. Be strongest where the field is level; avoid markets run by a "mafia".**
- Essay: powerful (2026).
- His reasoning: startups win by making the best product, which only pays off where customers decide on quality and decide quickly. Record labels and pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs) are examples where you may exist only as long as the mafia allows; beat them by coming in from the side and making them irrelevant. Customers who decide slowly (hospitals, school districts) are "walking through mud", so find a subset that decides faster.
- Rests on: P7, P15, P11 (examples are period-specific).

**13. A pure technical or quality lead tends to be erased by technology; when differences vanish, brand takes over, and he advises staying away from brand as a game to play.**
- Essay: brandage (Mar 2026).
- His reasoning: technology naturally makes substantive differences between products disappear; brand is what is left. Quartz made accurate timekeeping a commodity; surviving Swiss makers moved from precision to luxury signalling, with artificial scarcity and an asset-bubble-like business. Branding is centrifugal (must be distinctive) while good design is centripetal (right answers converge), so brand and quality pull apart. Quality becomes a threshold ("accurate enough"; the brand must not break character). He recommends not buying or selling brand, and instead to "follow the problems".
- Rests on: P7, P21.

**14. Startup returns are driven by a few huge winners, so judge ideas by the chance of succeeding very big, not by the chance of succeeding.**
- Essay: swan (2012).
- His reasoning: about three quarters of the roughly $10 billion value of YC's companies came from two (Dropbox, Airbnb); outcomes vary about 1000x. A safe-looking startup that succeeds modestly does not move investor returns. Whether others (Demo Day investors) fund an idea is a misleading metric, even inversely related. This is written from the investor's viewpoint; a founder who does not want to swing for the fences may legitimately choose otherwise (he says so in fn 1).
- Rests on: P9, P10, P13.

**15. Understand a specific set of users deeply; the key is empathy, not exploitation.**
- Essays: earn (2026), startupmistakes #10 (2006), before (2014), bronze (2005).
- His reasoning: you create wealth roughly in proportion to how well you understand the problem you solve. For users other than yourself you are "flying on instruments", so you must find specific users and measure. Expertise on startups (convertible notes, etc.) is not what matters; expertise on users and the problem is. Dale Carnegie is his recommendation for learning to see others' viewpoint.
- Rests on: P22, P12.

## 2. Concrete tests and numbers

| "Who wants this right now?" test | Ask who wants this so much they will use it even as a crappy v1 from a two-person startup they have never heard of. If you cannot answer, the idea is probably bad. | startupideas, 2012 | P22 |
| Activation-energy caveat | The needed urgency depends on switching cost. Enterprise software sold through traditional channels has very high activation energy (must be "a lot better"); switching search engines is low. | startupideas, 2012, fn 3 | P22 |
| Well test (breadth vs depth) | Choose "a small number of people want it a lot" over "a large number want it a little". Demand shape alone is "almost necessary, not sufficient"; also need a fast path out to adjacent users. | startupideas, 2012 | P22, P13 |

| "Why doesn't someone make x?" | In your previous job, did you ever wish someone made a particular thing that you would have bought immediately? If so you probably have an idea, because you know there is demand and it is buildable. | startupideas, 2012 | P22 |

## 3. Questions he would ask the founder

1. Who wants this right now, so much that they would use a crappy version one from two strangers? (startupideas, 2012)
2. Would you use this yourself if you had not written it, and have you ever said "why doesn't someone make x"? (startupideas, 2012, fn 14 and text)
3. Did you come to this idea by noticing something missing in your own life or work, or by trying to think of a startup idea? (startupideas 2012; organic 2010)
4. Which specific people, by name, have you talked into using this? (startupmistakes #10, 2006)
5. What Microsoft is this the Altair Basic of; if this works for your first small group, who is next and what is the path out? (altair 2015; startupideas 2012)
6. What do all the existing players overlook, and what thesis do you have that is more precise than "ours won't suck"? Did they fail to follow through on their own insight? (startupideas, 2012)
7. Which part of this idea do you secretly avoid because it is a schlep or because it is unsexy? What would you wish someone else would build? (schlep 2012; startupideas 2012)
8. What is your growth rate, and does it come from users telling friends? (earn, 2026)
9. What would make this company more powerful: do you own the customer relationship, does money (or tokens) flow through you, can others build on you, is there a network effect? (powerful, 2026)
10. In what ways is this idea held back by other companies, and in what ways by your own fear (the "just y" advice)? (powerful, 2026)
11. How big does a customer have to be before they buy, and could you sell to them earlier, or to a subset that decides faster? (powerful, 2026)
12. Are people using your product in ways you did not intend, and could the peripheral thing be the real product? (powerful, 2026)
13. Is this a market where the best product wins, or one dominated by gatekeepers (a "mafia") who decide whether you exist? (powerful, 2026)
14. If you had not already invested a month or a year in this, would it still be the thing people would pay most for? (bronze, 2005)
15. Is the aim to make money and maybe be cool, or the reverse? (bronze, 2005)
16. If this works, could it be a very big outcome (not merely a good one), and is it an idea few others think is good? (swan, 2012)

## 4. Failure patterns he warns about

1. **Sitcom or made-up idea.** A plausible-sounding idea for some other group of people (social network for pet owners; "portal for x"; local-event aggregator; recipe site). Friends say "maybe I could see using it". Zero real users once summed. (startupideas, 2012; startupmistakes #10 and #4, 2006)
2. **Model of the world that does not match reality (the Artix pattern).** Founders invent a theory of the market (galleries want to be online) and keep building for months without testing it on users. They know the product, not the buyers. (bronze 2005; startupideas 2012)
3. **Still life effect.** You take the first idea, plunge in, and at each point feel you have invested too much to switch. Fix: discount for invested time and revisit in the "harsh light of morning". (bronze, 2005)
4. **Doing something cool rather than something that pays; divided loyalties.** Keeping one foot in another world, or choosing an idea because it sounds cool. (bronze, 2005)
5. **Timidity: the marginal niche.** Picking a small, obscure niche to avoid competition ("hyena" strategy). You can only avoid competition by avoiding good ideas. (startupmistakes #3 and bronze, 2005-6)
6. **Derivative idea.** Imitating an existing company instead of starting from a problem. Few successful startups began as imitations. (startupmistakes #4, 2006)
7. **Schlep blindness and the unsexy filter.** Unconsciously rejecting ideas that involve payments, regulation, deals with banks, or distasteful domains; picking convenient-but-crowded ideas instead. Also "not wanting to get your hands dirty": hoping to just write code and have users appear. (schlep 2012; startupideas 2012; startupmistakes #16 2006)
8. **Attractive-to-fear ambition filter.** The biggest ideas repel you because they threaten your identity; the subconscious hides them. Counter: think of ideas for someone else, then approach obliquely. (ambitious 2012; startupmistakes #3 2006)
9. **Obstinacy, and its opposite.** Holding to the original plan when it is wrong, or switching ideas every week. Test: do the new ideas reuse what you built? (startupmistakes #5, 2006)
10. **Playing house.** Going through the motions: plausible idea, raise at a good valuation, rent an office, hire people, neglect making something people want. Look-for-the-trick mentality. (before, 2014)
11. **Optimising the wrong metric (investor eyes).** Evaluating ideas by what investors will think or whether others will fund them, rather than by whether users urgently want them. He says fundraising success can be inversely correlated with big-win potential. (swan 2012; before 2014)
12. **Asking "could this be a big company?" too early.** This filter excludes good ideas and attracts bad ones. Turn it off while noticing, apply it later. (startupideas, 2012)
13. **Treating a technical or quality lead as a durable edge, or retreating into brand.** Omega kept making more accurate movements while accuracy became a commodity; brand-selling is a bad problem to work on. (brandage, 2026)
14. **Component supplier in someone else's box.** Being a mere supplier of components without owning the customer relationship; selling only to big slow customers; operating in "mafia" markets. (powerful, 2026)
15. **Waiting too long to launch, or building before selling.** Launching slowly killed far more startups than launching early; excuses are procrastination. (startupmistakes #8, 2006; startupideas 2012 fn 15)
16. **Ignoring the user-benefit constraint.** Adding network effects or full stack because you want power, not because it helps customers. (powerful, 2026)
17. **Half-hearted effort.** Most common failure: a side project never given full effort; often because founders know deep down it is a bad bet. (startupmistakes #18, 2006)

## 5. How his view changed over time

- **2005 (ideas):** Startup ideas are close to worthless; treat the first idea as a question; look for broken things, luxuries made into commodities, easier-to-use technology; "design for exit" because 98% of the time success means getting bought, and make something several acquirers would want. The Woz route (hacks with friends) is mentioned as "the most productive way", but this is one among several methods.
- **2005 (bronze):** Smart people go through an "Artix phase" with bad ideas; key mistakes are the first idea, ambivalence about money, and timidity. He says choosing problems can be learned, and rejects the claim that a hacker needs a "business person" on the team.
- **2006 (startupmistakes):** Same core (make something users want), plus an explicit warning against marginal niches and derivative ideas, and the advice to leave the business model for later.
- **2008 (newthings):** Reframes his approach as a recipe that generates contemptuous initial reactions; the "crude version 1 delivered informally" form.
- **2010 (organic):** Introduces "organic" versus Viaweb-type ideas; says organic ideas are better, especially for young founders. Notes that he and Morris (30 and 29) could predict what users needed. In footnote 2 he says that in retrospect they should have become direct marketers, i.e. users of their own product. Soft claim here: the most successful startups are closer to the Apple type.
- **2012 (schlep, ambitious, startupideas, swan):** A big year. He adds the schlep filter and the unsexy filter; reverses the tone from "organic is better" to a fuller recipe ("live in the future, build what is missing"); adds the well shape of demand, the competition section (competitors almost never kill startups), and the idea that big ideas are frightening. Swan adds the investor-side view (1000x variation, best ideas look bad). In startupideas he stresses that the routes out from an initial idea are hard to see in advance, and that this is the main thing to understand about them.
- **2014-2015 (before, altair):** For students: do not start a startup in college; "just learn". The Altair question is posed as a puzzle for founders. He also admits he cannot predict who will be tough enough; no correlation between initial attitude and outcome.
- **2024 (google):** Compresses to three things (technology skill, idea, cofounders) and "build stuff and do well in school". Adds that your friends should really want it and "would be really sad to lose" it. Fn 2: this is just the best source for young founders, not the only one.
- **Mar 2026 (brandage):** A shift in register: not an idea-finding essay but a warning that technology erases substantive differences, after which brand rules. He advises avoiding brand games. Also "fields have golden ages"; follow problems.
- **Jun 2026 (earn):** Restates the organic recipe with a stronger arithmetic frame: growth rate x duration; your own needs predict future demand because you are young; "if you are consciously looking for startup ideas, it will make you too conservative." Mentions Airbnb: "we thought the idea was bad" and funded them for the founders (the 2026 essay is more explicit about this than swan 2012, which says the same in a different way).
- **Sep 2026 (powerful):** New in the corpus: advice shifts from "find the right problem and the beachhead" to "make the company powerful", with specific moves (marketplace, full stack, APIs for agents, token flow, etc.). It moves the center of gravity from product quality to position, though he says the constraint is that every such move must help the customer. It also adds the "mafia" market warning and the idea of selling to earlier-stage customers (Stripe) and the explicit advice to sell cheaply first and worry about margins later.
- **Overall drift:** from "have a good idea and make something users want" (2005-2012) toward "the idea is a starting point; what matters is the user, then sources of power" (2026). The earlier essays treat competition as an exaggerated fear; the 2026 essays treat position and lock-in as the real question.

## 6. Where his advice is time-bound

Marked: **[PG]** = from PG's own text; **[inference]** = my inference; **[ledger]** = from the era ledger's own notes (the ledger itself says its basis is "judgement", not verified against market data, except P7, P17, P22 which cite PG's own recent writing).

**P1 (building is cheap; amplified).**
- Affects: "launch fast/crude v1" (newthings, startupmistakes #8-9), Buchheit's "sell it before building" (startupideas fn 15), "an idea is worth nothing; the work is the question" (ideas 2005), "being good at technology" (google 2024).
- What changes: [inference] a working prototype now proves very little about demand or skill, so the "launch something fairly quickly" test is easier to pass but carries less information. A competitor can copy the crude v1 quickly. Selling before building gets even stronger. [PG] He already said in 2012 that ideas have no market; what is new is that the build step stops filtering founders. [ledger] "Building something is no longer evidence of commitment or skill."
- Specific: his "leading edge of programming in a year" advice (startupideas) assumes programming is the path to the edge; [inference] the edge may now be elsewhere (the domain you apply AI to).

**P4 (great programmers scarce; transformed).**
- Affects: startupmistakes #6 (hiring bad programmers kills business-guy startups), #7 (wrong platform), google 2024 ("get good at programming"), "technical founders can hear the voice of the customer" (bronze).
- [inference] Hiring-bad-programmers as a killer is much weaker where AI writes the code; the equivalent today is a founder who cannot judge what the AI produced. Platform choice matters less if code is cheap to port. [PG] In google 2024 he still says programming is the median startup source "for the next 10 years"; that was written in March 2024 and is not updated in these essays. [ledger] judgement and taste become the scarce things.

**P5 (software costs nothing per user; weakened).**
- Affects: powerful 2026 advice to "sell as cheaply as you need to at first; get all the users, then worry about margins", and the usage-based pricing advice for early-stage customers (Stripe).
- [inference] If each use costs compute, sell-cheap-get-users has a floor: heavy users can lose you money. His own caveat (fn 4: do not give away so much that growth stops being a signal) is the nearest thing in the text. [ledger] cost per user must be in the model from day one.

**P6 (distribution free if product good; weakened).**
- Affects: earn 2026 (growth from users telling friends is the core mechanism), startupideas (the activation energy idea), "make something people want and growth follows".
- [inference] If products flood channels, word of mouth alone is a weaker growth source, so his benchmark of "15% a month" may be harder to achieve and may be less informative. [PG] powerful 2026 hints at this indirectly by emphasising owning the customer relationship, APIs and agents as users. [ledger] "Doing things that don't scale and talking directly to users matter more."

**P7 (better product is a durable edge; weakened, PG has said so).**
- Affects: bronze ("the product is what wins" / newthings "the product is what wins in the long term"), startupideas (crowded market thesis), swan, and any test that rests on "build something clearly better".
- [PG] brandage 2026 argues technology erases differences; powerful 2026 shifts the question to power. [PG] In newthings (2008) he wrote that the product wins in the long term; the 2026 essays qualify this: quality becomes a threshold and position/brand/power decide the rest. This is the most explicit PG-side shift in the set.
- [inference] A pure quality or technical lead should be treated as temporary even in the early stage; the founder should name the source of power (relationship, data, network effect, standard, ecosystem) in addition to the better product. Also, brandage's own advice is "avoid brand" - which is in tension with a world where brand is what is left (see section 7).

**P2 (money can't make software faster; reversed for some).**
- Affects: bronze ("VC-backed startups are not that fearsome... too busy trying to spend all that money"), startupmistakes #11-13 (raise little, spend little, ramen), "competition is overrated".
- [inference] For companies that need compute or model training, a well-funded competitor is more dangerous than his 1995 e-commerce example; for application-layer startups the old premise mostly holds. [ledger] this is explicitly "reversed for some startups".

**P15 (big orgs slow; split).**
- Affects: startupideas ("competitors almost never kill startups"; HP turned down Wozniak), ideas 2005 ("you can't defeat a monopoly by frontal attack", "the Windows killer will come from a startup"), bronze (Open Market), ambitious (Apple will not produce the next great thing).
- [inference] Where the work depends on compute, data or distribution that incumbents have, "they are too slow" is a weaker assumption. [PG] powerful 2026 fn 1: "The question is how easy it would be for [model companies] to engulf you, or even your customers", which suggests he sees the supplier risk himself.

**P20 (cheaper failure means more attempts; amplified).**
- Affects: "competitors rarely kill startups" and "you may not be too late".
- [inference] If attempts are cheap and copies appear quickly, the claim that it is "exceptionally rare for startups to be killed by competitors" probably needs a re-test; the "do not worry that you are late" part is still plausible, but the thesis about what others overlook has to be stronger. [ledger] "Speed and power matter more than having the idea first."

**P22 (users decide; holds, with a new wrinkle: users may be AI agents).**
- Affects: all "who wants this" tests. [PG] powerful 2026 says to prefer APIs because "agents are replacing human users" and "who knows what they'll want to do". [ledger] build for agents as users too.

**P21 (taste, holds and more valuable).**
- Affects: startupideas ("if you are the right sort of person you have the right sort of hunches") and ideas (habits of mind). [inference] If generation is cheap, choosing which idea to chase becomes the main work, which makes the "prepared mind / own hunches" principle more, not less, important. Also note that PG gives no way to check taste besides "your hunches are better if you are at the leading edge".

**P9/P10 (investors herd; VC economics; hold).**
- Affects: swan 2012. [ledger] holds in logic; numbers (Dropbox/Airbnb share of YC value, 30% target, 94% rate in 2010) are historical. Use them as an illustration of reasoning, not as present data.

**P11 (specifics of a period; dated).**
- Affects: ideas 2005 design-for-exit (98% acquired, "kiko.com" style on-spec development), startupmistakes (Windows vs FreeBSD, Java applets, e-commerce in 1995, bubble platforms), ambitious's specific ideas (new search engine vs Google, replace email, universities, internet drama, next Steve Jobs, Moore's Law, ongoing diagnosis), bronze (1995, MIT spinoff claim), brandage background (Swiss watches), powerful examples (record labels, PBMs). [ledger] "Use these as examples of reasoning, never as current facts." [inference] The ambitious list is time-stamped 2012; several items (ongoing diagnosis, replacing universities, the next hardware company) have played out differently or are still open; treat as P18 (track record) rather than guidance.

**P18 (specific technology predictions; resolved).**
- Affects: ambitious 2012 and ideas 2005 predictions. [PG] mixed record in his own text (e.g. he thought Windows would be transcended by a startup via web apps in 2005, which broadly happened). [inference] treat as track record.

**P12/P13 (human nature, arithmetic; hold).**
- Most principles in sections 1 and 4 rest on these and carry over. P13: formulas hold, inputs may change (15% a month benchmark, 93% example).

**P14 (clustering in places; holds, contested).**
- Affects: startupmistakes #2 (location); google 2024 (best university for cofounders). [ledger] mostly holds; [inference] the university claim concerns selection by difficulty, which AI-driven gaming of applications (P16) might weaken.

**P16 (proxies get gamed; amplified).**
- Affects: before 2014 ("playing house"; gaming the system stops working), google 2024 (do well in school). [inference] Also relevant to pitches and prototypes: polished demos prove less.

**P8 (founder leverage; split).**
- Affects: startupmistakes #11-14 and swan. [ledger] depends on whether the company is capital-light or capital-heavy.

**P3 (few people can build it all; amplified).**
- Affects: single-founder warning (startupmistakes #1) is mostly about emotional support, so probably holds; the "hire only coders or people who get users" advice gets stronger. [inference].

**P19 (technology makes pleasures more addictive; amplified).**
- Relevant to the "toys" and "social network" ideas; [inference] his "toy" heuristic should be tested against whether the thing is merely addictive; this is my link, not his in these essays.

## 7. Limits and tensions

1. **Organic ideas versus the survivorship problem.** Almost every example is a success story (Apple, Microsoft, Google, Facebook, Dropbox, Airbnb), chosen after the fact. He admits he cannot predict which ideas will have a path out ("the meta-fact that these are hard to see") and that the right method is "have the right hunches", which is not testable. (startupideas 2012)
2. **"Looks lame" is both evidence and noise.** Swan says the good ideas look bad and the vast majority of bad-looking ideas are bad; startupideas says being dismissed as a toy is "positive evidence" and also says to be wary of ideas that merely sound plausible. There is no rule for telling a good lame idea from a bad one except "real users love it". (swan 2012, startupideas 2012, altair 2015)
3. **Made-up ideas are bad, yet Viaweb was one.** His own organic.md concedes Viaweb was the second type (made up from afar) and still succeeded, and fn 2 says they should have become direct marketers. He explains this by his and Morris's age (30, 29) and experience; startupmistakes #10 repeats that you can build for others "but you are stepping into dangerous territory". (organic 2010, startupmistakes 2006)
4. **Noticing versus "give yourself time".** He says to look for problems and not to "think up" ideas, but then gives "recipes" (ask what you wish someone would build, look for waves, dying industries) for people who need an idea now, and warns that recipes produce more bad ideas than the organic method. Section titles "Recipes" and "Organic" in startupideas make the ranking explicit, but the risk of self-deception is not resolved.
5. **Schlep advice versus "make it cool".** startupideas says to turn off the unsexy filter yet also says excitement about an idea means you will work harder on it (fn 13: he concedes you cannot expect to spend all your time on what interests you most). bronze says "where there's muck there's brass" and warns against cool; startupideas ends with "live in the future and build what seems interesting". These can be reconciled (the second is for finding ideas, the first for filtering them) but he does not say how to weigh them.
6. **Competition is overrated versus lock-in matters.** Competitors "almost never" kill startups (2012), yet powerful 2026 treats being engulfed by suppliers and gatekeepers as a main risk. He does not reconcile how the "ignore competitors" advice sits with the "ask whether you could be engulfed" fn 1 of powerful.
7. **Product quality versus power.** newthings (2008: "the product is what wins in the long term") and powerful (2026: startups make the best stuff, so they are strongest on level playing fields) say quality wins where customers decide on quality; brandage and powerful say quality is a temporary edge and power must be built. He gives no test for how quickly a quality lead decays in a given market.
8. **Brand: avoid, yet it is what is left.** brandage concludes you should stay away from brand (even from selling it, "it's harder than it looks") because it is a bad problem to work on, while describing it as the force technology naturally produces. For a founder in a commodity market this leaves unclear what to do; his answer is "follow the problems", i.e. move to a field with interesting problems, which is not a business strategy for existing companies. The essay is also explicitly about luxury goods, so how far it transfers to software is inference.
9. **Small start versus "frighteningly ambitious".** Start with deceptively small (ambitious, altair); also the biggest ideas are the ones you should be scared of. His resolution is to approach obliquely, but the advice "do not say you will replace email" is tactical and does not address whether the big goal should guide choices. He also says (ambitious) it may be best to have a "blurry" view of the future.
10. **Founder advice versus investor logic.** swan advises choosing ideas for chance of succeeding big; before and bronze say to take it step by step; startupmistakes says investors often impose caution. He notes in swan fn 1 that founders do not have to swing for the fences. The investor and the founder face different payoffs, and many of his "go big" lines are partly about YC's portfolio.
11. **Age.** Organic and earn say young founders should build for themselves (their needs predict demand); before says "do not start a startup in college" and that you cannot know if you are up to it; startupideas says it gets harder to switch careers as you age; bronze notes older founders (29, 30) also fell for a bad idea. Older founders' route is less specified: "stand outside yourself", "talk to everyone", "act like consultants" (fn 16 sorority example, he says "I have no idea whether this would work").
12. **Evidence is thin or anecdotal in several places.** Examples: "most successful startups begin organic" (no counts), 98% acquired (2005, no source), "no correlation between initial attitudes and outcomes" (impression, not data), startupmistakes fn 13 admits the day-job claim is a guess; "ideas that seem bad but are good" is from a small set of famous cases; the 30% fundraising figure is a thought experiment he says YC would never follow.
13. **Where he flags his own uncertainty.** he says he is not certain a social network for pet owners is a bad idea (startupideas fn 2); "I have no idea whether this would work" (fn 16); "I don't know if this one is possible" (ambitious, new search engine); "I'm guessing here" (startupmistakes #18); he warns that most such reworkings of an idea lead nowhere (powerful).
14. **The list of power moves is not ranked and may conflict.** Open source and APIs (give control away) versus owning the customer relationship and making money flow through you (hold control); generosity versus "money flows through you". He says all must benefit the customer, but gives no way to choose between them or to tell when giving away stops being generous and becomes a donation.
15. **Time and the "prepared mind".** He advises taking time to notice ideas (and says few founders will wait, so the best ideas are undervalued), but a founder with a deadline or a short runway has no good answer in these essays beyond the recipes; the "sell before you build" trick (fn 15) is the quickest test in the set.
