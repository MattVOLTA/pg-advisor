# Idea lens

Is this idea worth years of someone's life, and what would make the company hard to beat? Graham's tests for whether demand is real have held up well. His view of what makes a startup defensible has changed, and he changed it himself in 2026.

If you haven't already, read the pg-advisor `SKILL.md` (at `../pg-advisor/SKILL.md` relative to this skill's base directory) for the answer format, time check and citation rules. The full source material, with numbers checked against the essays, is in `references/source-pack.md`. Read it for the full list of power moves, failure patterns and tensions.

## Work through these

### 1. Where did the idea come from?

Graham's strongest claim (How to Get Startup Ideas, 2012; Organic Startup Ideas, 2010): the best ideas are noticed, not thought up. They come from a problem the founder has, ideally while living at the leading edge of something. Made-up ideas sound plausible and have no real users. He calls them "sitcom" ideas.

Ask: did you notice this in your own life or work, or did you go looking for a startup idea? If it's for other people, which named people have you talked into using it (2006)?

This rests on human nature and users deciding (P12, P22: holds).

### 2. Who wants this right now?

His test (2012): who wants it so badly they'd use a crappy first version from two strangers? Better a few people who want it a lot than many who want it a little. Then: is there a path out from that first group to a bigger market? His question for lame-looking ideas (2015) is: what Microsoft is this the Altair Basic of?

Holds (P22). **Time check (P1, P16):** building a first version is now nearly free, so "we built it" proves little. The test that still carries information is whether someone uses or pays for it. The fastest test he passes on is Paul Buchheit's (in a footnote to the 2012 essay): go and sell the product before building it. Founders learn quickly whether anyone wants it, and often find the real idea while trying. That test is now even more useful.

### 3. Is it being avoided for a reason?

Schlep blindness (2012): founders unconsciously avoid tedious or unpleasant problems, so those ideas stay open. Stripe took on payments, with banks, fraud and regulation. The unsexy filter works the same way. Both are positive signs if real users want the result.

**Time check (judgement, not Graham's):** AI makes some schleps cheap. Paperwork, data entry and integration code are easier than they were. The schleps that still protect a company are the ones AI doesn't remove: regulation, liability, trust, physical operations, slow-moving customers, messy integrations with other organizations. Ask which kind of schlep this is.

### 4. What about competitors?

Graham's long-standing view (2006, 2012) is that competitors rarely kill startups. A crowded market is fine if you have a precise thesis about what the incumbents overlook, not just "ours will be better". The exception is a competitor with lock-in.

**Time check (P7, weakened, and Graham says so; P20 amplified):** in The Brand Age (2026) he argues that technology tends to erase real differences between products, after which brand takes over. In Making Startups Powerful (2026) he moves the question from "how is it better?" to "what makes it more powerful?" Copies now appear faster and cheaper (P20). Treat a quality or technical lead as temporary, and require an answer to step 5.

Where the product depends on frontier models or compute, incumbents and model providers are a real threat (P15, split). Graham himself asks in 2026 how easily the model companies could swallow you or your customers.

### 5. What would make it powerful?

His 2026 list: own the customer relationship; have money flow through you; let others build on you; create network effects (including users opting in to help train your model); go full stack; notice when a side feature is the real product; sell to early-stage customers who decide fast and grow with you; get the data early; offer APIs, because AI agents are increasingly the users. His constraint is that every move must make things better for the customer, or it won't work.

Ask the founder to name at least one source of power they could plausibly build. If they can't, say so plainly.

### 6. Is the founder chasing fashion?

"Do something in AI" is not an idea. Graham warns against ideas chosen because they sound cool or impressive (2005) and against thinking up ideas rather than noticing them. The question is whether AI makes a specific problem newly solvable for specific users. If it does, that's a good idea. If AI is just the label, it isn't.

## Tensions to be honest about

- **Survivorship bias.** His examples of lame-looking ideas that won are all survivors. Most lame-looking ideas are just bad. The only reliable tiebreaker he gives is real users loving it now.
- **Competition.** "Competitors rarely kill you" (2012) and "could you be engulfed?" (2026) are not reconciled. Lean on the 2026 view where models, compute or gatekeepers are involved.
- **Brand.** He says to stay away from brand, while describing it as what's left once technology erases differences. For a founder in a commoditising market, the practical answer is to build power, not brand.
- **Investor framing.** Swing-for-the-fences reasoning (Black Swan Farming, 2012) is partly portfolio logic. He says himself that founders don't have to aim for a giant outcome.
